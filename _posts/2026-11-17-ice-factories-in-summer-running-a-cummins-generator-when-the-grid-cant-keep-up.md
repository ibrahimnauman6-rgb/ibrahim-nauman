An ice plant runs a production cycle around the clock, and every cut sets that cycle back.

## How a block-ice plant uses power

The big load is the refrigeration compressor, driven by a large motor that runs for long hours. Around it sit the brine agitator that keeps brine moving past the ice cans, the condenser water pumps, the cooling tower fans, the crane or hoist that lifts the cans, and the filling and thawing equipment. Lights and the office are small by comparison.

Most of these run continuously while the plant is making ice. That turns an ice plant into a prime-power job during outages, not a short standby one. When the grid is off for hours at a stretch, the set is effectively the plant's power station, so size it on its prime rating under ISO 8528 and not on the higher standby figure.

## What a power cut does to the freezing cycle

When the compressor stops, the brine starts to warm and the agitator stops pushing it past the cans. A short cut sets the batch back. A long one can mean blocks that need much longer in the tank, or come out softer than buyers at the harbour will accept.

Owners tell us an outage tends to cost more time than its own length, because the system has to pull the brine back down before freezing picks up properly again. That's the real argument for backup in this business: protecting the cycle, not just keeping the lights on.

There's a restart question too. A refrigeration compressor restarting before pressures have settled can be hard on the motor, and on whatever is supplying it. Plants usually have an unloading arrangement or a set start procedure. Ask your refrigeration contractor for it, and paint it on the wall beside the panel so the night shift follows it every time.

## Sizing a Cummins generator for the compressor start

On most ice plants the size comes down to one moment: the main compressor motor starting. Direct-on-line starting pulls several times the running current. Star-delta starting or a soft starter cuts that a good deal. Find out which your compressor uses, then give the motor rating and the starter type to whoever is sizing the set.

Then sequence it. Bring the condenser pumps and cooling tower fans up first, start the compressor on a set that is otherwise lightly loaded, then add the agitator and the hoist. One big start at a time keeps the voltage dip manageable.

If the plant has two compressors, ask whether the set really has to run both. Sometimes one compressor on backup is enough to stop the brine losing too much ground during an outage, and the second waits for the grid. That single decision can change the size of the set a lot.

Compressors on variable speed drives behave differently again. They start more gently, but they put harmonics on the supply, so tell your supplier about any drive before the alternator is chosen.

## Salt air on the coast, dust inland

Coastal plants in Karachi, Gwadar and Pasni put a Cummins generator in humid, salty air, which is hard on radiator cores, alternator windings and electrical terminals. A well-sealed canopy, regular radiator cleaning and an anti-condensation heater in the alternator are all worth asking about.

Inland plants in Sukkur or Rahim Yar Khan face 45°C afternoons and dust instead, which pushes the cooling system hard in a different way. Wherever the plant is, keep the set out of the splash zone around the tank and the can-thawing area, and route the condenser water drains away from its plinth. Ice plants are wet places, and an alternator doesn't like standing water.

## Fuel for long summer runs

A set carrying a compressor for many hours a day burns a lot of diesel. Work out a realistic daily figure with your supplier, using the engine's fuel curve at your expected load rather than a brochure number at full load. Then size the day tank and the bulk storage so a late tanker can't stop production.

Keep fuel records: hours run, litres added and the load at the time. Diesel prices move often, so cost your outage hours at the current rate each season instead of reusing last year's sums. If you'd like a quote for a set, ask for it against your load list and not just a kVA figure.

## Service intervals arrive fast

Running hours pile up quickly in a long summer. A set doing many hours a day can reach its service interval in weeks rather than months. Do a full oil and filter service before the season, book the mid-season one in advance, and keep a filter set and coolant on site so a due service never waits on a parts run.

Follow the intervals in the operation manual by hours run, not by the calendar. On a Cummins generator working an ice plant's summer, the hour meter tells you far more than the date does.

Run this way, the set stops being a June worry and simply becomes part of the plant.
