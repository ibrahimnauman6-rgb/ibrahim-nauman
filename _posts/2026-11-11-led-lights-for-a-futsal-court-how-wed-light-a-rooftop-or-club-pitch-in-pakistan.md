## Why so many futsal courts are lit badly

Futsal has taken off in Pakistani cities. In Lahore, Karachi and Islamabad you'll find courts on the roofs of commercial plazas, on empty plots behind schools and inside sports clubs, and most of them do their busiest trade between 9 pm and 2 am, once the summer heat finally drops. That means the LED lights are carrying the part of the day the whole business depends on.

At Hyundai LED we get asked to quote these courts fairly often, usually by the contractor putting up the netting and the turf. We build most layouts from our ++[weatherproof flood lights and street lights](https://hyundailedlights.com.pk/outdoor-lights/)++, because a court is an outdoor space that takes rain, dust and late-night dew. Floods cover the playing area, and a street light or two looks after the parking and the walkway in.

The trouble is that most courts get lit the way a boundary wall gets lit: a few big floods on the corners, aimed at the middle. Players then spend the evening squinting at a ball that vanishes into glare.

Here's how we'd plan the LED lights instead.

## Start with the players' eyes, not the wattage

Futsal is a low, fast game. The ball lives at knee height, and the goalkeeper spends the match looking straight down the court or up at a chipped shot. Any flood in that line of sight will blind somebody.

So our first rule is simple. No fitting should be aimed so that a player looking along the length of the court sees the bright face of the lamp. In practice that means mounting along the long sides rather than behind the goals, and tilting the floods further down than an electrician's first instinct.

Wattage comes after that. Two poles carrying clusters of high-watt floods will light the court, but they'll throw hard shadows too. More fittings at moderate wattage along both sides give a more even spread, and light to spare if one fails on a tournament night.

## Pole height and positions on a rooftop

Rooftop courts bring their own problems. The parapet is usually low, the slab may not like heavy poles bolted into it, and the nearest houses are often a street's width away.

Before we suggest a layout, we ask the contractor for three things:

- the court dimensions and where the netting frame sits
- whether the floods can go on the net frame's steel columns or need their own poles and bases
- what's on the far side of each wall: a road, a house, a mosque or an empty plot

Fixing floods to the netting frame is common and it can work. But that frame was designed to hold netting, not fittings plus their wind load, and in a May andhi the whole structure moves. Get whoever fabricated it to confirm it can take the extra weight first.

## Keeping light out of the neighbours' windows

This is the complaint that gets courts shut down. A flood aimed outward at a shallow angle throws light into bedrooms across the street, and in Johar Town or Gulshan-e-Iqbal that reaches the society office fast.

Aim everything inward and downward. If a fitting has to sit near an edge that faces houses, mount it higher and point it more steeply down, so the beam lands on the turf instead of spilling over the road.

Then check it at night from the street before the opening match. Ten minutes on the pavement tells you more than any drawing.

## Circuits, switching and load-shedding for the LED lights

Split the court lighting into at least two circuits. That way the owner can run half the floods for a warm-up or a small booking and the full set for a proper match. It trims the bill and gives you a fallback when a breaker trips.

Most courts trade straight through load-shedding hours. If the owner has a generator, put the floods on the changeover and make sure the set can pick them up along with the fans and the canteen fridge. If there's no backup, tell him plainly that bookings stop when the feeder goes.

Our outdoor floods use wide-voltage drivers, which helps with the swings you get on a busy evening feeder. A wide-voltage driver still isn't a surge arrester, though. On an exposed rooftop, a surge protection device at the distribution board is cheap insurance.

## Heat, dust and the rated working temperature

Here's a detail people skip. Our outdoor fittings list an operating range up to 40°C, and a Multan or Sukkur rooftop in June goes well past that by mid-afternoon. The saving grace for a futsal court is that the lights mostly run at night, when the air has cooled down.

Even so, keep floods off hot roof sheets and out of airless corners, and switch them off by day. Heat is what wears out the drivers in LED lights early.

Dust is the other enemy. After an andhi the glass on a flood can carry enough grit to dull its output. Wipe the fronts every few weeks in the dusty months, with the circuit off, and check for water behind the glass after the first monsoon rain.

## What to hand over to the court owner

When the job's finished, give the owner a single page showing which breaker runs which lights, the model of each fitting, where it's mounted and its warranty. Courts change managers a lot, and that sheet saves the next person a night of guessing.
