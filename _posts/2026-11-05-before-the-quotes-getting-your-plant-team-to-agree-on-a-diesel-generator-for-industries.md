## Why a diesel generator for industries needs more than one signature

Here's a pattern we at Enpower see again and again. A factory decides it needs backup power, someone in purchase sends out an enquiry that says "250 kVA, silent, urgent", and three weeks later there are four offers on the table that can't be compared with each other.

The offers aren't the problem. The brief is. Buying a diesel generator for industries touches production, the electrical team, maintenance, admin and finance, and each of them knows something the others don't. When only one of them writes the enquiry, the rest of the plant finds out what was missed after the set is on its foundation.

So before you ask anyone for a quote, we'd suggest getting five people to put their part on paper. It takes a week, maybe less. It saves months.

## Production: what can't stop, and how quickly it has to come back

The production manager is the only person who can say which loads truly matter when LESCO or FESCO trips at 2 p.m. In a Faisalabad spinning unit that might be the ring frames and the humidification plant, because yarn quality suffers when the hall dries out. In a Multan cold store it's the compressors and the evaporator fans. In a Karachi plastics unit it's the extruder heaters, since a cold barrel means a long restart.

Ask production to write down three things for each critical line. How long can it sit without power before you lose product? Can it restart in steps, or does everything start at once? And how many hours a day did it actually run on backup last June?

That last answer matters, because it decides the duty the set is bought for. A diesel generator for industries that covers twenty minutes of tripping is a different machine from one that has to run half a shift every day through a Sukkur summer.

## Electrical: what the new set has to plug into

Your electrical in-charge knows the building in a way no dealer does on a first visit. Get them to list the existing panels, whether you already have generators that the new one might need to share load with, and how changeover happens today. Is it a manual switch someone runs to, or an automatic transfer?

They should also flag the big motors. Chillers, air compressors and large pumps pull a heavy current at start, and a set that looks generous on a running-load list can stumble on the first big motor start. We'd rather hear about the 75 kW compressor on day one than find it during commissioning.

If you run VFDs or a large UPS, note that too. Those loads change how the alternator should be chosen, and it's a conversation worth having early.

## Maintenance: who lives with the machine after the dealer leaves

Maintenance usually gets asked last, which is odd, because they're the ones who'll change its filters for years. Their list is practical. Which engine brands do we already know? Do our technicians have the service tools for this make? How far is the nearest stocked parts counter from our gate?

In Pakistan that last point is bigger than it sounds. Parts are imported, the dollar rate moves, and a filter that's "available" might mean a two-week wait. Maintenance should ask every bidder where parts are held and who will turn up when the set won't start on a Sunday.

## Admin and HSE: where it sits and what it does to the neighbours

The site plan is admin's job, and it shapes the whole spec. A set beside the labour colony or a neighbouring office needs a proper acoustic canopy and an exhaust that points somewhere sensible. A set in an open yard in Hyderabad or Multan has to cope with andhi dust and direct sun on the canopy, so ask about air intake filtration and where the hot air goes.

HSE will want to see where the day tank and any bulk fuel storage will go, how spills are contained and how far the set is from anything that burns.

## Finance and purchase: comparing offers on scope, not just the total

Finance doesn't need to understand alternators. It does need a fair comparison, and that only works if every offer covers the same scope. Before the enquiry goes out, purchase should list what must be included: delivery to site, offloading, foundation work, cabling to the main panel, the changeover panel, commissioning and a load test.

Ask every bidder to price against that list and to show what's excluded. A cheaper offer that leaves out cabling and commissioning isn't cheaper. We'd also tie payment milestones to real events, such as delivery and a witnessed load test, rather than to the calendar.

## Putting it on two pages before you call anyone

Once each department has written its part, merge it into one short brief. Critical loads and run hours. Motor starts. Existing panels and sets. Site, noise and fuel. Service expectations. Scope of supply. Two pages is plenty.
