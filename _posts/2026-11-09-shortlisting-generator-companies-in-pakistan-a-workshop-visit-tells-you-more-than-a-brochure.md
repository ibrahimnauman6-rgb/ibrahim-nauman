Most buyers we meet at Enpower have already done the obvious search before they call anyone. They've got a list of generator companies in Pakistan, a handful of websites that all say "authorised", "24/7 support" and "genuine parts", and no real way to tell them apart.

That's not a dig at anyone's website, ours included. Websites are written to sell. The useful information sits in places a brochure never shows: the workshop, the parts store, the paperwork from a past job, and the customers who've had a breakdown. Here's how we'd build a shortlist if we were sitting on the buyer's side of the table.

## What generator companies in Pakistan actually are

The word "company" covers very different businesses. Some are authorised dealers, appointed by an engine or generator brand for a territory, with access to factory parts, technical bulletins and warranty claims. Some are importers or distributors who bring in complete sets and sell through others. Some are local packagers who buy an engine, an alternator and a controller separately and assemble the set here. And some are traders who deal in whatever stock is available, including reconditioned units.

None of these is wrong for every job. A trader may be perfectly fine for a short-term site. But for a factory, a hospital or a cold store that will lean on the set through years of load-shedding, you want to know which kind of business you're talking to. It decides who stands behind the engine when something fails.

## Check the authorisation, then check it again

Ask for the authorisation letter or certificate and read it properly. Whose name is on it? Which brand issued it, for which products, and does it carry a date or a territory? A letter in a different company's name, or one that ran out years ago, tells you something on its own.

Then confirm it with the brand, not the seller. Many engine makers list their appointed dealers on their own websites, and most will answer an email asking whether a company is on their list. It takes ten minutes. It's also the cheapest check in the whole process.

While you're at it, ask what the company is authorised to do. Selling a brand is one thing. Being able to lodge warranty claims and order factory parts against an engine serial number is what you'll care about in year three.

## Go and see the workshop

We'd always suggest a visit before anyone makes the shortlist. Not the showroom, the workshop. Generator companies in Pakistan that have something to show will usually welcome you, and an hour there teaches you more than a week of emails.

Look at who's working. Are the technicians on the company's payroll, or called in job by job? Are there service vehicles with tools in them? Is there somewhere to run a set on load before it leaves, or a load bank for testing? Ask how a breakdown call gets logged and who decides which technician goes out.

Then ask to see the parts store. Filters, belts, sensors and gaskets for the engines they actually sell, on labelled shelves with part numbers, is a good sign. Empty racks and "we'll order it when you need it" means your next breakdown will include a shipping wait.

## Ask for a commissioning report from a past job

This one surprises people. Ask each company for a commissioning report from a recent installation, with the customer's name blanked out if they'd rather. It shows you exactly what they test before handing a set over.

A good report records the checks before first start, readings at no load and at stepped loads, voltage and frequency, oil pressure and coolant temperature, the changeover test, and who signed it off. If all you get is a single line saying "set started OK", that's roughly how your own handover will go.

## Make the reference calls that matter

Every company has happy customers to offer. Ask for something harder: a customer whose set had a fault, and a customer in your own city. Support based in Lahore doesn't help much if your plant is in Sukkur or Rahim Yar Khan.

When you call, skip "are you satisfied?" and ask what happened the last time the set broke down in the middle of summer. How long until someone arrived? Was the part in stock? Did the same technician come back, or someone new every time? People tend to be honest when the question is specific.

## Put the after-sales promises in writing

"24/7 support" doesn't mean much until it's in the purchase order. Ask every shortlisted company to write down its response time for your location, what the warranty covers and for how long, who pays travel to an out-of-city site, and what a maintenance contract includes once the warranty ends.

Also ask who does the work under that contract. Some firms subcontract service in other cities, which is fine if they say so up front and stand behind it. It's less fine when you find out on the day of a breakdown.

## Score the shortlist before the prices arrive

By now your list of generator companies in Pakistan should be down to two or three names worth asking for an offer. Before the quotes land, agree inside your own team how you'll weigh them. Price matters, of course. So do the authorisation, the parts on the shelf, the quality of the commissioning paperwork and what the reference customers told you.

A simple score sheet with those headings, filled in before anyone sees a number, stops the cheapest offer from winning by default. It also gives your finance team a written reason if you don't pick the lowest one.
